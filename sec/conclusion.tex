\section{Conclusion}

This work investigated whether modern transformer-based 3D encoders capture the topological structure of shapes. Using DONUT, a controlled benchmark specifically designed for this purpose, we quantified how much these models understand intrinsic topological features such as connected components and genus. Across architectures and analyses, results consistently showed that current 3D transformers lack a clear and consistent understanding of topology. Their representations reflect geometric and semantic cues poorly encode the structural invariants that define the global organization of a shape.

A more detailed probing revealed that global structural information tends to emerge more distinctly in the deeper blocks of the encoders, suggesting that higher layers progressively integrate coarse structural relationships. In contrast, fine-grained topological signals, captured through persistent homology, appear to be distributed across all layers regardless of depth, indicating that local structural cues are already present throughout the hierarchy. However, the interpretation of these latter results remains challenging, as CKA scores show strong variability depending on the chosen vectorization of persistence diagrams, which introduces additional uncertainty in assessing the precise extent of topological encoding.

Overall, these findings highlight a broader limitation of current 3D representation learning methods. When moving beyond curated datasets toward real-world 3D data, models often face shapes that lack stable geometric parametrizations or explicit semantic labels but share common topological patterns. In such settings, architectures that overlook topology risk missing crucial structural information. Integrating topological data analysis with deep learning, as explored in this work, offers a principled avenue toward representations that capture not only what a shape looks like but also how it is fundamentally organized.
